I can build you the 3D model — but I should be upfront first: a magnet wheel like this can't actually power a house or run forever. It's a perpetual motion machine, which violates conservation of energy. Magnets store no net usable energy per cycle — for every stretch where a magnet pulls the wheel forward, there's a matching stretch where it has to pull it back, so the net work over one rotation is zero (magnetic fields are conservative). PETG is the best all-round choice. The load itself is trivial — a phone stand only has to resist ~150–250 g of mostly static weight, which any common filament handles easily — so the deciding factor is heat: PLA softens around 55–60°C, which a stand near a sunny window or in a car can reach, causing it to sag under the phone's weight. PETG's heat deflection is ~70–80°C, it's tougher (won't crack if knocked), and it prints almost as easily as PLA (no enclosure/ventilation needed like ABS, which is stronger on heat but more prone to warping and fumes). **Verdict:** ❌ won't work as designed for the spin/extraction phase — tap water supplies tens of watts, spin-drying needs 300–500 W, roughly a 10× shortfall. ⚠  The slow tumble/wash-agitation phase is plausible at small scale, but only by running tap water continuously through the machine for the whole cycle, which wastes far more water than it saves in motor cost.

**Understanding:** Goal is a domestic clothes washer with no electric drive motor — the drum is turned entirely by a small turbine fed from the mains tap. Assumed: standard home mains pressure (2–4 bar) and a single tap/supply line feeding it (not a dedicated high-pressure pump), and that "washing machine" means the normal wash + spin-dry cycle, not just a wash tub.

**How it works (conventional machine):**
- Wash/tumble phase: drum turns slowly (~40–55 rpm), reversing periodically, lifting and dropping wet laundry. Needs moderate torque, low speed → tens of watts.
- Spin phase: drum accelerates to 800–1400 rpm so centrifugal force (up to ~280 g at 1000 rpm, r = 0.25 m) throws water out through the perforated drum. This is the power-hungry phase: overcoming air drag, bearing friction and the wet load's own inertia at high speed for several minutes.
- A motor supplies both phases from mains electricity (~3 kW circuit available), so it never runs short of power.

**In the proposed design:** a turbine (Pelton/Turgo-style wheel, most likely) replaces the motor, driven by the pressure drop across the tap. Water either goes to drain after passing the turbine, or into the drum as wash water.

**Numbers:**
- Hydraulic power available: P = Δp × Q × η. Typical mains 3 bar, 12–15 L/min at a fully open tap (known/typical), small turbine efficiency ~50–60% (estimated) → P ≈ 3×10⁵ Pa × 2.5×10⁻⁴ m³/s × 0.6 ≈ **45 W**. Pushing every assumption generously (6 bar, 20 L/min, 70% efficient turbine) still only gives ≈ **140 W** (estimated, upper bound).
- Power needed to spin-dry: **300–500 W** (known, typical washing-machine spec).
- Shortfall: roughly 3–10×, and that's before adding the extra torque needed just to accelerate the wet drum from 0 to 1000 rpm in a reasonable time (kinetic energy term, not counted above).
- Water needed if the spin phase is turbine-driven: to sustain 45–140 W for a typical 5–8 minute spin means 15–20 L/min flowing continuously = **75–160 L used just for spinning**, on top of wash and rinse water. A standard machine uses **40–60 L for the entire cycle** (known, reference value). So this design would use 2–4× more water than a whole normal wash, for the one phase it can't even power properly.
- Wash/tumble phase, by contrast, needs only ~20–30 W (estimated: 6 kg wet load, radius 0.2 m, 45 rpm, torque ~5 N·m) — comfortably inside the 45–140 W turbine budget.

**Problems (worst first):**
1. **Spin-dry doesn't work.** Power is 3–10× short, so the drum either won't reach extraction speed or will take an impractically long time while burning far more water than the entire wash cycle needs.
2. **A turbine only makes power while water flows.** Unlike a motor, there's no way to store energy — every minute of rotation costs litres of tap water, whether or not that water is doing anything useful (most of it would just go to drain). This is the opposite of water-efficient, which undercuts the likely motivation for the idea.
3. **No reverse/hold/variable-speed control.** Wash cycles reverse direction periodically and modern machines ramp spin speed gradually with imbalance detection; a raw turbine drive gives one direction and whatever speed the water pressure happens to produce, with no easy way to pause mid-spin without dumping the kinetic energy.
4. **Mains pressure is not reliable.** It varies through the day, by location, and is gravity-fed (<1 bar) in some homes/countries — a design that depends on it for a core function will behave very differently house to house.
5. **Mechanical mismatch.** An efficient small impulse turbine wants a bucket speed around half the jet velocity (~12 m/s at 3 bar); coupling that to a drum at 45–1000 rpm at a sensible radius needs a gearbox, which adds cost, friction losses (eating into the already-thin power budget), and another wear/seal point.
6. **No precedent.** Tap water is cheap, ubiquitous, and manufacturers are highly incentivized to cut the motor (their most expensive single part) if it worked — the absence of any such product on the market is itself evidence against it.

**Better option:** Keep the tap-turbine only for the tumble/wash-agitation phase (where the numbers actually work), and drop the motor-free spin-dry idea. For water extraction, use gravity drain plus a hand-crank or foot-pump centrifuge (as some off-grid/camping washers do), or accept a longer drip-dry. This keeps the "no electric motor" goal without the 100+ L water penalty.

**Improvements (if you still want to pursue the turbine-tumble version):**
1. Use the turbine only during fill/wash, not spin — biggest single fix, removes the worst water-waste problem.
2. Route the turbine's spent water straight into the drum as the wash fill, so the power isn't "extra" water — it's water you needed anyway. Trade-off: couples flow rate to wash-fill rate, so agitation speed isn't independently adjustable.
3. Add a simple flow-diverter valve to reverse the jet against the turbine buckets (or a ratchet/cam mechanism) for direction reversal. Trade-off: added mechanical complexity for a wear part.
4. Size the turbine wheel/runner radius so its efficient tip speed matches drum rpm directly (avoid a gearbox). Trade-off: less flexibility, and the sweet spot only holds for one pressure/flow condition.

**Questions:**
- Is water cost/usage a real constraint, or is minimizing electricity specifically the goal even at the cost of extra water? Default assumed: both matter, since "no motor" for a washing machine is usually about off-grid/low-power use, where water is also often limited (rules out the water-hungry spin-dry version entirely).
- Is this for a specific low-resource/off-grid context (camping, disaster relief, no electricity) or a mains-connected home appliance? Default assumed: general domestic use with normal mains water pressure.

**Next:** If you want to go ahead with the wash-only turbine version (drop motorized spin-dry, add manual/gravity water removal), I can size the turbine, gearing and drum for that and sketch it out — say the word and I'll build it.
