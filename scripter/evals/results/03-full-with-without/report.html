    cube([wall, width, wall + lip_height]);

**Verdict:** ⚠  works with changes — the mechanism is sound (it's a real, self-locking scissor mechanism), but two things aren't fixed by your sketch alone: the leg-splay angle (which sets seat height *and* stability, and 45 cm legs can't give you both a normal chair height and a stable footprint) and how the canvas is anchored (the usual failure point on this style of stool).

**Understanding:** You want a 3D version of a classic X-frame canvas stool: two boards crossing on a central pivot bolt, canvas strap spanning the two top ends, legs 45 cm (known/given). Side view only, so width, splay angle, and canvas attachment are unshown and need to be inferred.

**How it works:**
- Each leg is one straight rigid piece, pivoting on a shared bolt at its midpoint — so the top end and foot of each leg are always on opposite sides of that one point, same distance out.
- The canvas at the top is inextensible. Once it's taut, you have a triangle with three fixed side lengths (pivot→top1, pivot→top2, top1→top2) — by simple geometry (SSS), a fixed-length triangle can't change shape. So a taut canvas strap doesn't just sit there decoratively — it's what locks the whole frame rigid and stops the feet from splaying out under your weight. No canvas tension = no rigidity = it folds flat. This is the actual mechanism behind this style of stool, not just an aesthetic detail.
- Load path when someone sits: body weight → canvas tension pulling the two top ends together → each leg loaded roughly as a compression strut from foot through the pivot → bolt in bearing/shear at the crossing point → feet on the floor.

**Numbers** (45 cm leg length is the only *known* value; everything else below is derived from an *assumed* splay angle of 35° from vertical, chosen to balance seat height against stability):
| Quantity | Value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Leg length | 45 cm | known |
| Pivot position | midpoint, 22.5 cm from each end | derived (required for a symmetric X) |
| Splay angle from vertical | 35° | assumed |
| Seat height | 45·cos35° ≈ 37 cm | derived |
| Front-back footprint / taut canvas span | 45·sin35° ≈ 26 cm | derived |
| Stool width (left-right) | 28 cm | assumed |
| Leg board section | 28 cm × 1.8 cm thick | assumed |
| Pivot bolt bearing stress (80 kg sitter) | ≈3.4 MPa on an M8 bolt through 1.8 cm wood | estimated — comfortably under wood bearing strength (~5–10 MPa), fine |

If instead you want a more "normal" chair-height stool (~43–45 cm seat height), the splay angle drops to ~17°, which shrinks the front-back footprint to ~13 cm — too narrow to be stable or to fit a canvas seat wide enough to sit on. **With 45 cm legs you get one or the other, not both**: this is why real camp/fishing stools of this leg length sit low (~33–38 cm), not chair height.

**Problems (worst first):**
1. Nothing but canvas tension holds the stool open — there's no mechanical stop shown. That's normal for this stool type, but it means the canvas *attachment* (not the fabric itself) is the weak point: stapled/tacked canvas pulling out of end-grain is the classic failure mode on cheap versions of this design.
2. Seat-height vs. stability trade-off above — needs your input.

**Better option:** Manufactured versions of this stool usually use two separate narrow X-frames (left and right) joined by two cross rails at the top, with canvas stretched between the rails rather than stapled to bare board ends — lighter, and the rail gives the canvas a proper anchor. Your one-bolt, single-wide-board version is simpler to build (fewer parts, matches your sketch exactly) and is a legitimate, real design — I'd keep it unless you want to shave weight.

**Improvements:**
- Attach canvas as a sewn-and-looped pocket around each top end (or a small dowel through each top) rather than staples/tacks — same part count, much less prone to pulling out. Trade-off: slightly more sewing work.
- Round/chamfer the top edges the canvas wraps over, so it doesn't get cut by a sharp corner under load.

**Questions:**
1. Seat height priority — keep it low & stable (~37 cm, my default) or prioritize chair height at the cost of a narrow, tippier footprint?

// generic cylinder-between-two-points helper, reused for legs and the bolt
function makeCylinderBetween(a, b, radius, material) {
  const dir = new THREE.Vector3().subVectors(b, a);
  const length = dir.length();
  const geo = new THREE.CylinderGeometry(radius, radius, length, 16);
  const mesh = new THREE.Mesh(geo, material);
  mesh.position.copy(a).add(b).multiplyScalar(0.5);
  // align cylinder's default Y-axis with the direction vector
  mesh.quaternion.setFromUnitVectors(new THREE.Vector3(0, 1, 0), dir.clone().normalize());
  mesh.castShadow = true;
  return mesh;
}

// two X-leg assemblies, front and back

function buildSeat() {
  const lengthSegs = 24, widthSegs = 3;
  const positions = [], indices = [], uvs = [];
  for (let i = 0; i <= lengthSegs; i++) {
    const u = i / lengthSegs;
    const x = -w + u * (2 * w);

    for (let j = 0; j <= widthSegs; j++) {
      const v = j / widthSegs;
      const z = zFront + v * (zBack - zFront);
      positions.push(x, y, z);
      uvs.push(u, v);

      const b = a + 1;
      const c = a + (widthSegs + 1);
      const d = c + 1;
      indices.push(a, c, b, b, c, d);

  geo.setAttribute('uv', new THREE.Float32BufferAttribute(uvs, 2));

# Waterwheel Washer: A Motor-less, Mains-Pressure-Driven Washing Machine

The core idea: replace the electric drive motor with a small **impulse turbine** (Pelton/Turgo wheel) fed by tap pressure, and use the *same* incoming fill water that a normal washer wastes across a pressure-reducing inlet valve to do mechanical work instead. Below is a full concept, including the two problems that actually make this hard: **power budget** and **backflow safety**.

## 1. Power budget (the binding constraint)

Household mains: ~40–80 psi (0.3–0.55 MPa), ~10–20 L/min through a standard supply line.

Hydraulic power available: `P = ΔP × Q`

- 60 psi (413 kPa) × 12 L/min (0.0002 m³/s) ≈ **83 W** of hydraulic power
- At ~70% turbine efficiency → **~55–60 W mechanical**, better in high-pressure areas (up to ~150–200 W)

That's roughly 1/5 to 1/10 the power of a normal washer motor. It's workable, but only if you spend it wisely:

- **Wash/tumble** (~40–60 rpm, low torque): easily covered by 50–80 W.
- **Spin/extract** (800–1200 rpm): tighter. Kinetic energy needed to spin up a drum+load (I ≈ 0.5 kg·m²) to 1000 rpm ≈ 2.7 kJ. At 60–80 W that's roughly **40–90 seconds** of spin-up if transmission losses are kept low — acceptable, but it means spin can't be instant like a motor-driven machine, and low-pressure homes may need a flywheel assist (see §3).

## 2. Turbine choice

**Pelton/Turgo wheel** (impulse type), not a Francis/axial reaction turbine — because household supply is high-pressure, low-flow, which is exactly the regime impulse turbines are built for, and they tolerate pressure fluctuations well (a nozzle just converts pressure to jet velocity; buckets extract momentum regardless of exact upstream pressure).

- ~8–10 cm wheel, stainless or glass-filled nylon buckets
- Inline strainer ahead of the nozzle (mandatory — grit/scale will chew up bucket edges fast; hard water will scale the nozzle orifice)
- A single fixed nozzle is fine; a needle valve on the nozzle gives crude speed control

## 3. Drivetrain

```
[Mains valve] → [strainer] → [nozzle] → [Pelton wheel] ─┬─ shaft ─┬─ [wash gearset]  (~40:1, reversing) → drum
                                                          │         │  via Scotch-yoke/reversing dog clutch
                                                          │         └─ [spin gearset]  (~2:1) → drum
                                                          │            via centrifugal or timer-actuated dog clutch
                                                          └─ [recirc pump impeller] (separate shaft extension,
                                                              submerged in sump — see §4)
```

- **Wash mode**: high reduction ratio + a reversing mechanism (Scotch yoke or ratcheting pawl clutch) turns the turbine's constant rotation into the oscillating tumble action classic top-loaders use.
- **Spin mode**: a timer-actuated dog clutch disengages the wash gearset and engages a much lower-reduction (near direct) path, and opens the nozzle to full flow.
- A **flywheel** on the turbine shaft, engaged during wash, is worth adding — it banks kinetic energy over the (longer, low-power) wash phase, then a clutch dumps that energy into the drum at spin time, shortening the water-hammer of a cold spin-up in low-pressure homes.
- A simple flyweight (centrifugal) governor throttling the nozzle is a cheap way to cap spin speed and stop wasting water once target rpm is hit — same trick used on hydro-turbine governors for a century.

## 4. The water-reuse loop (and why it must be hydraulically separate)

Continuously drawing fresh mains water for the whole wash cycle would blow way past a normal washer's total water use (~50–100 L/cycle) — a 10-minute wash at 12 L/min alone is 120 L. So:

- **Mains water only drives the turbine during fill and spin** — phases where water is already being added/removed anyway. Spent turbine water free-falls into the tub and becomes the wash/rinse bath (no extra waste — this is the trick that makes the concept water-neutral-ish for those phases).
- **During the wash/soak hold**, the mains valve is throttled to a trickle (just enough to keep the turbine barely turning for tumble power), and a **second, separate impeller** on the same shaft recirculates sudsy sump water through its own spray manifold to keep clothes agitated without drawing fresh water.
- **Critical**: the recirc loop and the mains-fed turbine must be two *physically separate water circuits*, coupled only by the shared shaft — never sharing water. The turbine discharges through an **air gap** into the tub, exactly like a standard washer inlet funnel. This is a plumbing-code requirement (cross-connection control): sudsy/graywater must never have a path back into the potable supply. Don't cut this corner even though it'd be "simpler" to submerge one turbine in the sump.

## 5. Drain and controls — keep the "no motor" ethos consistent

- **Gravity drain** (standpipe below the machine) avoids needing an electric pump. If the install can't provide fall, a **venturi eductor** off the mains jet can assist draining — costs extra water, adds no moving parts.
- **Timer/sequencer**: a mechanical rotary cam timer (clockwork or water-ratchet driven) sequencing valve position — Fill → Wash(trickle+recirc) → Drain → Rinse Fill → Spin(full flow, high-ratio clutch) → Drain. This lets the whole machine run with **zero electricity** if you want — genuinely off-grid, usable anywhere with mains pressure and no power.
- **Heat**: skip the electric heating element; just plumb in the house hot tap for warm washes. Wash temperature is capped at whatever your water heater delivers.

## 6. Honest limitations

- **Water consumption is materially higher** than an efficient electric washer, since water is now the working fluid for both mechanical power *and* cleaning — expect noticeably more L/cycle unless the recirc loop is doing most of the wash-phase heavy lifting.
- **Performance depends entirely on your house's water pressure/flow.** Low-pressure or well/pump-fed homes, or homes with flow restrictors, will get sluggish tumble and long spin-ups; the design should specify a minimum supply (e.g., 40 psi / 8 L/min) below which it just won't perform.
- **No water pressure = no wash**, full stop — a real tradeoff against power-outage resilience (this machine is immune to blackouts, but not to water shutoffs or drought restrictions on flow).
- **Spin speed/time is modest** compared to a motor (tens of seconds to ~1–2 minutes to reach speed at typical pressures), so expect wetter laundry out of the spin cycle unless a flywheel-assist is added.
- **Backflow prevention isn't optional** — needs a code-compliant air gap/backflow preventer at the mains inlet regardless of the internal separation described above.
