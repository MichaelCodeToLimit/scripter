// (extrusion axis below = width = vertical print axis, no supports needed)

width       = 80;   // mm, overall width / linear_extrude height
wall        = 3;    // mm, plate thickness (base, back panel, lip)

slot_depth  = 12;   // mm, gap between lip and back panel (phone + case thickness)
front_margin = 3;   // mm, extra base length behind the panel's pivot (stability)

horizontal_reach = back_length * cos(back_angle);
base_depth = wall + slot_depth + horizontal_reach + front_margin;
pivot_x    = wall + slot_depth;

module profile2d() {
    union() {
        // front lip
        square([wall, lip_height]);

        // base plate
        square([base_depth, wall]);

        // angled back support panel, pivoting from the base
        translate([pivot_x, 0])
            rotate([0, 0, back_angle])
                square([back_length, wall]);
    }
}

**Verdict:** ❌ won't work as designed — PLA softens below normal wash temperatures, and even at room temperature the hoop stress from the spinning wet load is close to or above what a printed PLA shell can take.

**Understanding:** Goal is a 3D-printable drum: same 1200 rpm spin, PLA instead of steel, diameter doubled (~50 cm → ~100 cm). Assumed: drum depth stays ~32 cm (not stated), so capacity isn't preserved — it's a side effect of the diameter change (see below).

**How it works:** During spin, wet laundry is flung against the drum wall by centrifugal force; the wall must hold that load in hoop tension (like a pressure vessel), and it must do so at wash temperature, not room temperature.

**Numbers:**
- Drum wall "g-force" at r = 0.5 m, 1200 rpm: g = ω²r/9.81 ≈ 804 g (known formula; ~2× the original 402 g at r = 0.25 m, since g-force scales linearly with radius at fixed rpm).
- Wet laundry load: 7 kg dry cotton retains ~2–2.5× its weight in water before spin extraction → but at double diameter with same depth, drum volume is ~4× (volume ∝ r²), so realistic capacity is closer to 25–28 kg dry, ~60–70 kg wet (estimated).
- Hoop stress from wet load pressing on wall, 5 mm PLA wall: σ ≈ P·r/t ≈ 55 MPa (estimated, assumes uniform load distribution — conservative but order-of-magnitude correct).
- PLA bulk tensile strength: 50–70 MPa (known). FDM interlayer (Z-axis) strength: typically only 30–45 MPa of that (known) — the layer lines run exactly across the hoop-stress direction in a printed drum.
- → Safety factor ≈ 0.5–1.3 at room temperature. Marginal to failing before heat is even considered.
- PLA glass transition / heat deflection under load: ~50–55°C (known). Wash cycles run 30–90°C (known). Above ~55°C PLA goes rubbery and loses most of its strength — a 40°C "warm" cycle is already at the edge, a 60°C or 90°C cycle is well past it.

**Problems (worst first):**
1. Thermal: any warm-or-hotter wash cycle exceeds PLA's heat deflection temperature; the drum would sag/creep under its own spin load, independent of the stress question.
2. Structural: even cold, the hoop stress from the wet spin load is at or above realistic printed-PLA strength — safety factor near 1, no margin for wear, impact (zips, buttons) or fatigue.
3. Fatigue/impact: PLA is brittle with poor layer adhesion; 1200 rpm = 20 Hz, so a 15-minute spin is ~18,000 load cycles, repeated every wash over years — delamination risk at the print layers.
4. Long-term water exposure: PLA hydrolyzes (degrades) with repeated soaking, especially at wash temperatures — strength drops further over the machine's life.
5. Printability: a ~100 cm drum exceeds typical desktop printer build volume (~20–30 cm); it would need to be printed in segments and bonded, and those seams sit right where hoop stress is highest.
6. Unstated consequence: doubling diameter alone (same depth) roughly quadruples drum volume — a ~25 kg-capacity machine, not a 7 kg one. Motor torque, bearing, suspension and frame all sized for 7 kg are then undersized too.

**Better option:** Keep PLA for a non-functional display/scale model (no water, no load, low or no spin) if the goal is a printable showpiece. For a working drum, either keep it steel, or if 3D printing is a hard requirement, use a higher-temperature engineering filament (PA/nylon, Tg ~50–90°C depending on grade, much better fatigue and impact resistance) and keep the diameter increase modest — but this still needs real stress/thermal validation, not just a material swap.

Turbine-driven spin mechanism — design:

## Architecture
Mains water → strainer → pressure-regulating valve → solenoid control valve → nozzle(s) → **Turgo runner** (impulse turbine — best fit for household head/flow: unlike Pelton it accepts a jet at an angle so the runner can sit inline with a compact shaft, and unlike a reaction (Francis/Kaplan) design it doesn't need a submerged sealed casing under pressure) → runner shaft → centrifugal clutch → drum shaft → turbine exhaust routed into drum tub (doubles as rinse water) → existing pump/drain.

Keep the turbine decoupled from wash agitation. Impulse turbines are a poor fit for the low-speed/high-torque agitate cycle (that's torque-dense, turbines are speed-dense), so let your existing agitation mechanism handle wash, and give the turbine one job: spin/extract.

## Turbine sizing
Nozzle jet velocity:
```
v = Cv * sqrt(2 * ΔP / ρ)
```
Cv ≈ 0.97–0.98 for a clean converging nozzle.

Runner efficiency peaks near:
```
u_runner ≈ 0.46 * v   (Turgo, single-stage)
```
Pick runner pitch diameter D so that the shaft speed at that peripheral speed lands near your desired direct-drive spin rpm — this is the lever that avoids a gearbox:
```
ω = u_runner / (D/2)
```
If your ΔP/flow numbers put optimal runner rpm above target drum rpm (likely — impulse runners tend to run fast at modest heads), step down with a single-stage belt or planetary rather than gearing up; a step-down is more efficient and tolerant of the debris/wear environment than a step-up would be.

Use multiple nozzles (2–4) around the runner if you need finer control authority at part-flow — lets you modulate torque by valving individual nozzles instead of throttling one, which keeps jet velocity (and thus efficiency) constant across the speed range.

## Speed control / ramp profile
- Hall-effect or optical sensor on the drum shaft, closed loop to the solenoid valve(s) — impulse turbines have no back-EMF-style natural speed regulation the way a motor does, so without closed-loop control an unloaded/lightly-loaded runner will overspeed on mains pressure alone.
- Ramp profile: low flow (one nozzle) for initial distribution/balance phase, full flow for extraction phase, valve closes and clutch disengages for coast-down — don't rely on turbine braking, it won't have any.

## Coupling
Centrifugal clutch between runner shaft and drum shaft: engages once runner reaches a threshold rpm, freewheels below it. This keeps the turbine from being dragged/loaded during wash agitation and keeps the agitator motor from having to spin the turbine runner as dead weight.

## Water routing
- Strainer immediately after the mains tee — tap water debris will wreck impulse buckets over time, this isn't optional.
- Turbine casing is unpressurized on the discharge side (impulse, not reaction), so the exhaust can dump straight into the tub — route it there and you've folded your fill cycle into your spin cycle for free.
- Backflow preventer (RPZ or equivalent) at the mains connection — you're now plumbing a powered path that can see detergent-contaminated water; most plumbing codes will require this regardless of your own risk assessment.

## Safety
- Door interlock cuts the solenoid valve, not just the drum lock — a pressurized jet spinning an unlocked drum is the failure mode to design out first.
- Standard out-of-balance switch on the tub suspension, same as any spin cycle.
- Pressure relief upstream of the regulator in case the solenoid fails closed.
