Building a chair mechanism from scratch (tilt, gas lift, base) also isn't a sensible DIY project; the fix is picking/adjusting the right dimensions and features, possibly retrofitting what you have.

**Understanding:** Goal is to stop back pain from desk sitting. I don't know your height, current chair, or desk setup (**assumed**: average adult, existing chair may be non-adjustable or set up wrong). I don't know if you want to *buy* a chair, *retrofit* your current one, or *DIY-build* one from wood/foam/printed parts.

**How it works:**
- Your spine has a natural S-curve. Sitting without lumbar support lets the lower back flatten (posterior pelvic tilt), which raises pressure inside the lumbar discs and stretches the ligaments behind them — this is the main mechanical driver of ache.
- Separately, holding *any* position for a long time requires small postural muscles to stay contracted continuously. That cuts local blood flow and causes fatigue pain regardless of chair quality — this is why pain shows up around the 1–2 hour mark even in good chairs, and why movement matters as much as the chair itself.
- A chair fixes the first problem (support) but not the second (staticness) — that needs breaks or a sit-stand routine.

**Numbers** (known/estimated from ergonomics literature, verify against your own body):
- Seat height: 42–48 cm, set so feet are flat and knees ~90–100° (known typical range).
- Seat depth: 2–3 finger-widths of gap behind the knee when back is against the backrest (known).
- Lumbar support height: roughly 15–20 cm above the seat pan, matching your lumbar curve (estimated, varies with torso length).
- Recline: 100–110° backrest angle measurably lowers disc pressure vs. sitting bolt upright at 90° (known, classic Nachemson pressure studies) — sitting upright unsupported can run ~140% of standing disc pressure; reclining with lumbar support drops that substantially.
- Armrest height: elbows relaxed at ~90°, forearms roughly level with the desk.
- Movement: postural muscle fatigue builds over tens of minutes of static load — a break or position change every 30–45 min is the usual guidance, independent of chair design.

**Problems (worst first):**
1. Unknown current setup — can't size anything without your height and current chair type/adjustability.
2. Even a perfect chair won't remove pain if you never change position for 2 hours straight.
3. Full mechanical office-chair design (synchro-tilt, gas cylinder, 5-star base) is a solved, mass-manufactured problem — reinventing it from scratch is high effort for no gain over buying/adjusting one.

**Better option:** Rather than building a chair mechanism, either (a) reconfigure your current chair to the numbers above if it's adjustable, or (b) buy a chair built around those numbers, and pair it with a movement habit. If budget-limited, a DIY lumbar cushion (foam block or 3D-printed/carved insert) can retrofit a bad chair cheaply and addresses the single biggest lever (lumbar support).

**Improvements (ranked):**
1. **Lumbar support at the right height** — biggest single lever for disc pressure; trade-off: needs to match your torso, so it's not one-size-fits-all.
2. **Seat height + depth correct for your legs** — fixes knee/hip angle and blood flow behind the knee; trade-off: requires knowing your height/inseam.
3. **Recline to ~105° with backrest tension** instead of sitting bolt upright; trade-off: needs a desk/monitor height that still works reclined.
4. **Armrests at elbow height** — offloads shoulder/neck, which often refers pain into the upper back; trade-off: cheap armrests are often the wrong height and make it worse.
5. **Scheduled movement (stand/walk every 30–45 min)** — addresses the static-fatigue mechanism the chair can't fix; trade-off: needs a reminder/habit, not a chair.
