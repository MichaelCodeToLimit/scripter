**Verdict:** ⚠  works with changes — back pain after ~2h is almost always a lumbar-support + static-posture problem, not something that needs a chair built from scratch. A spec/retrofit fixes it in most cases; only go to custom fabrication if nothing off-the-shelf fits your body.

**Understanding:** Goal — stop back pain from sitting ~2h at a desk. Assumed (flag if wrong): you want a chair *specification* to buy or retrofit, not a from-scratch build; standard desk height ~73–76 cm; adult of roughly average height (~170 cm) until you tell me otherwise.

**How it works (the load path):**
- Your spine has a natural S-curve — the lumbar (lower back) section curves inward (lordosis). Sitting tends to roll the pelvis backward, flattening or reversing that curve.
- A flattened lumbar curve shifts load from bone onto the spinal discs and forces your erector spinae muscles and ligaments to hold the position isometrically for hours. That sustained, low-level muscle tension — not any single event — is what produces the ache around the 2-hour mark.
- A chair's job is to intercept part of that load: the seat pan carries your pelvis/thigh weight, the backrest (via lumbar support) carries part of the trunk's bending moment so your muscles don't have to, and armrests carry part of your arm/shoulder weight so your trapezius doesn't.
- No static posture is sustainable indefinitely — even a "perfect" chair needs to let you move (recline, shift) or you'll still stiffen up.

**Numbers (known ergonomic standards):**
- Seat height: 40–53 cm adjustable range to fit most adults, so thighs sit roughly horizontal and feet stay flat on the floor. Knee angle ~90–110°.
- Seat depth: 40–46 cm, ideally adjustable — you want a 2–3 finger gap between the seat edge and the back of your knee. Too deep = you either slide forward (losing back support) or the front edge cuts into your popliteal area (behind the knee), compressing blood flow and nerves.
- Lumbar support: should sit at L3–L5, roughly 15–23 cm above the compressed seat surface, and ideally adjustable in height and depth (people's torso lengths vary a lot).
- Backrest recline: working range ~100–110° from horizontal reduces spinal disc pressure noticeably versus sitting bolt upright at 90° (classic finding from in-vivo disc-pressure studies). A *dynamic* (synchro-tilt) mechanism that lets seat and back move together as you shift is better than a fixed angle, because it keeps trunk muscles gently active instead of fully passive or fully rigid.
- Recline mechanism load: for a ~70 kg person, roughly 60% of body mass (~40 kg) loads the backrest, at a lever arm of ~0.4 m from the pivot to shoulder height → torque ≈ 40 kg × 9.8 m/s² × 0.4 m ≈ 155 N·m at full recline. This is why tilt tension needs to be adjustable to your body weight — under-tensioned and the back flops, over-tensioned and it won't move, defeating the point.
- Armrests: height-adjustable so shoulders stay relaxed (not shrugged or drooping) and elbows sit near 90°; width/pivot-adjustable is a bonus if you type with arms angled in.

**Problems with a typical fixed/basic desk chair (worst first):**
1. No adjustable lumbar support, or a lumbar bulge in the wrong position for your torso length — the single biggest driver of the pain you're describing.
2. Fixed backrest angle (no recline/tilt) — forces one static posture for the whole session.
3. Fixed seat height/depth — if it doesn't match your leg length, you get thigh or knee-back pressure that indirectly causes you to slouch to compensate.
4. Fixed or absent armrests — shoulder/neck load creeps down into upper back tension over hours.
5. No prompt to move — even a great chair won't stop pain if you never change posture for 2 hours straight.

**Better option:** Rather than building a chair from raw materials (a serious furniture-engineering project — load-bearing joints, gas-lift certification, casters rated for your weight), the standard and far cheaper fix is either (a) a chair with the adjustments above, correctly *dialed in*, or (b) retrofitting your current chair with an adjustable lumbar cushion + correcting seat height, which solves most of this for under $50.

**Improvements, ranked:**
1. **Adjustable lumbar support, positioned correctly** — biggest single fix. Trade-off: none really; cheap add-on cushions work almost as well as built-in mechanisms if positioned right.
2. **Dynamic recline/tilt with weight-matched tension** — lets you unload the spine periodically instead of holding one position. Trade-off: adds mechanical complexity/cost (~$50–150 more).
3. **Seat height + depth set correctly for your leg length** — free if adjustable, but requires knowing your measurements.
4. **Height-adjustable armrests** — offloads shoulder/neck, indirectly reduces upper-back compensation. Trade-off: minor cost, can get in the way at a low desk if not also depth-adjustable.
5. **A movement habit** (stand or shift posture every 30–45 min) — no chair fixes prolonged static sitting on its own; this costs nothing and compounds with the above.

A few quick questions would help me tailor this, but here's a solid starting framework for a desk chair that won't wreck your back after 2 hours:

**Likely root cause**: Most "my back hurts after ~2 hours" complaints trace to one of these:
- No lumbar support (lower back rounds out, load shifts to spinal discs)
- Seat pan too deep/shallow (forces slouching or edge-perching)
- Armrests wrong height (shoulders shrug or you lean)
- Seat height wrong relative to desk (wrists/shoulders compensate, torso twists)

**Design spec for a better chair:**

1. **Lumbar support** — adjustable both in height and depth, contoured to sit in the small of your back (roughly belt-line). This is the single biggest fix for most people.
2. **Seat pan** — depth should let you sit back fully with 2–3 fingers of gap between the seat edge and back of your knees. Waterfall front edge (curves down) to avoid cutting circulation behind the knees.
3. **Recline mechanism** — synchro-tilt (seat and back move at different ratios) with a locking range around 95–110°. Slightly reclined is easier on spinal discs than bolt-upright.
4. **Adjustable armrests** — height, width, and ideally depth/pivot — so forearms rest at ~90° without shrugging shoulders, and elbows stay close to torso.
5. **Seat height** — adjustable so feet are flat on the floor (or footrest), knees roughly level with or slightly below hips.
6. **Headrest** (optional) — useful if you recline periodically to unload the spine.
7. **Material/cushion** — firm foam that doesn't compress flat after an hour; breathable mesh back helps if you run warm.

**Cheaper interim fixes before building/buying a whole new chair:**
- A lumbar cushion (e.g., rolled towel or a $20 lumbar pillow) on your current chair
- Set a timer to stand/stretch every 30–45 min — even a great chair won't fully offset 8 hours static
- Check monitor height (top of screen at eye level) — neck strain often masquerades as back pain
