Evidence (what the judge was shown; for an image, a description of what was sent)
**Verdict:** ⚠  works with changes — a single "device" isn't the right frame here; the reliable fix is a layered system, with a passive texture deterrent doing most of the work and a motion-triggered gadget only as backup.

**Understanding:** Goal is to keep a cat off kitchen counters for a full unattended workday (~9 hrs). Assuming (a) you want whatever actually works, not necessarily a single gadget, (b) one cat, and (c) a typical counter run with 1–2 jump-up points (e.g., a chair or stool near the counter).

**How it works (why cats jump up, and why each countermeasure does or doesn't hold up):**
- Cats go up for smell (food residue), warmth, height/vantage, or boredom. Any fix that doesn't address the *drive* just gets tested repeatedly.
- Physical barriers only work if they block every entry point — chairs, stools, adjacent shelves — which is hard in a real kitchen.
- Texture aversion (upside-down carpet runner nubs, or double-sided tape strips) works because cats dislike the paw sensation, not because they're scared. This means no habituation over time — it stays effective indefinitely, unlike fear-based deterrents.
- Motion-triggered air/spray deterrents (e.g., SSSCAT-style) work by startling the cat at the moment of the jump, but only in the narrow cone the sensor covers, and they run out of propellant with use.
- Ultrasonic deterrents rely on the cat being bothered by the sound at all — many aren't, and even responsive cats often habituate within days to weeks. Weak as a sole solution.
- Removing the motivation (no food smells, nothing to investigate) is free and doesn't need power or unattended operation to keep working.

**Numbers (available vs. needed):**
- Counter run: assumed ~8–10 ft (estimated, typical galley counter) — this is the area that needs 100% coverage while you're out 9 hrs/day.
- Texture strips: ~$10–15 total, zero power, covers full run, zero maintenance — needed uptime is 9 hrs/day unattended; texture aversion provides that with no failure mode (known).
- Motion-spray unit: covers roughly a 3 ft cone from one fixed point (known, typical PIR+spray device spec). To cover a 10 ft run you'd need 3 units ≈ $120–150 (estimated), and each canister is good for roughly 100–200 triggers (estimated) — if the cat tests the boundary 5–10×/day, that's a canister swap every 2–4 weeks. That's a real maintenance burden for something meant to run unattended.
- Ultrasonic: ~$15–20/unit, but effectiveness data is weak and habituation is common — don't rely on this as the primary layer.

**Problems (worst first):**
1. Any single-point device (motion spray, ultrasonic) leaves gaps — a cat that finds one unmonitored spot on a 10 ft counter will just use that spot.
2. Spray canisters need periodic refilling; "unattended all day" is fine day-to-day, but the system silently fails once a canister empties, with no alert.
3. Food smells/dirty dishes left on the counter undercut any deterrent — the cat has a reason to keep trying even if occasionally startled or annoyed.
4. Blocking the counter without giving the cat another high place just relocates the boredom/vantage-seeking behavior somewhere else (fridge top, shelves).

**Better option:** Layered, mostly passive system rather than a single powered gadget:
1. Texture deterrent (nub-side-up carpet runner or double-sided tape strips) across the *entire* counter surface — this is the unattended-proof baseline, since it needs no power, no refills, and doesn't habituate.
2. Keep counters clear of food residue/dishes overnight and before leaving — removes the motivation, free.
3. Give the cat an approved high perch (cat tree/shelf near a window) — redirects the height-seeking drive instead of just fighting it.
4. Optional: 1 motion-spray unit aimed at the one main launch point (e.g., the chair the cat uses to jump up), as reinforcement — not as the sole line of defense.

**Improvements (ranked):**
1. Full-surface texture strips first — biggest reliability gain per dollar, no unattended-operation failure mode. Trade-off: looks a bit unsightly until the cat stops testing it (usually 1–2 weeks), then can often be removed from most of the run.
2. Remove attractants — free, addresses root cause. Trade-off: none, just habit change.
3. Add a alternative perch — takes more space/cost (~$40–100 for a decent cat tree) but produces a more contented cat overall, not just a blocked one.
4. Motion-spray only at the specific jump point, not across the whole counter — cuts cost from ~$150 to ~$40–50 for one unit, accepting you still need the texture strips to cover the rest.

**Questions:**
1. Do you want a literal device/gadget built, or the most effective real-world solution even if it's mostly passive (tape/texture + perch)? Default: the effective practical system above.
2. Roughly how long is the counter run, and is there one main jump-up point (chair/stool) or several? Default: ~8–10 ft, one main jump point.
3. Just one cat? Default: yes — multiple cats would mean scaling up perch space and possibly covering more counter area.
